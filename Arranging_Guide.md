Arranging a jazz chord melody on guitar comes down to one fundamental principle: **the melody line must sit as the highest active voice at all times, with harmony and bass supporting it below.**

To build a compelling arrangement, players rely on a structured set of harmonic and textural techniques.

---

### 1. Harmonizing the Melody (Harmonic Techniques)

#### Drop-2 & Drop-3 Voicings

These are the foundational harmonic shapes for jazz guitar. They take standard closed-position 7th chords and drop specific voices down an octave to make them physically reachable on the fretboard.

* **Drop-2:** Drops the 2nd highest voice of a closed chord down an octave. Very compact and usually played on string groups 4-3-2-1 or 5-4-3-2. Excellent for fast, stepwise melody lines.
* **Drop-3:** Drops the 3rd highest voice down an octave. Features a wider spread with a distinct gap between the bass/root note and the upper voice cluster (e.g., played across strings 6-4-3-2). Great for anchoring downbeats.

#### Shell Voicings & "Three-Note" Harmony

You do not need to play 4-note or 5-note chords on every single melody note—doing so bogs down the rhythm. Shell voicings consist of:

* **Root + 3rd + 7th** (or just **3rd + 7th** under the melody note).
Because the 3rd and 7th define the chord quality (Major, Minor, Dominant), stripping out the 5th and root opens up space, makes fingerings nimble, and lets the melody sing cleanly.

#### Voice Leading & Passing Chords

To connect target harmony chords smoothly along a melodic line:

* **Diminished 7th Passing Chords:** Place a diminished 7th chord on non-diatonic or chromatic melody notes between scale steps to create tension/resolution (e.g., Barry Harris rules of movement).
* **Chromatic Approach Chords:** Move your primary chord shape up or down a half-step into the target chord.
* **Tritone Substitutions:** Replace a dominant chord (e.g., $G7$) with a dominant chord a tritone away ($D\flat7$) when the melody note aligns with the sub's extensions.

#### Upper Structure Extensions & Alterations

When the melody lands on a color note (9ths, 11ths, 13ths, or altered $b9, \sharp9, b5, \sharp5$), choose voicings that leave out lower chord tones (like the root or 5th) to house the extension on the top string.

---

### 2. Texture & Arranging Approaches

| Style / Technique | How It Works | Best Used For |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Block Chords (4-Way Close / Shearing Style)** | Parallel movement where every single melody note receives a 4-note voicing directly beneath it. | Fast swing passages, bebop lines, and shout choruses. |
| **Counterpoint / Two-Voice Motion** | Playing just two independent lines: the melody on top and a moving bass line or inner counter-melody below. | Ballads, rubato intros, and sparse contrapuntal sections (e.g., Ted Greene / Jimmy Wyble style). |
| **Chord-Bass Alternation (Walking Bass)** | The thumb plays a walking bass line on beat 1-2-3-4, while the fingers comp chords and melody on or off the beat. | Medium-to-up-tempo swing standards (e.g., Joe Pass / Tuck Andress style). |
| **Pedal Tones & Guitarlike Open Strings** | Holding a static bass note (often an open E, A, or D string) while shifting upper melody chords above it. | Creates rich, orchestral resonance during intros or transitions. |

---

### 3. Step-by-Step Method to Arrange a Lead Sheet

1. **Map the Melody First:** Play the tune using *only* single notes on the top two strings ($E$ and $B$). If the melody drops lower than string 2, transpose it up an octave so you leave lower strings open for harmony.
2. **Identify Target Notes:** Highlight the principal melody notes that fall on strong beats (beats 1 and 3). These are the anchor points where full chords belong.
3. **Harmonize Targets:** Choose a Drop-2, Drop-3, or shell voicing where the top note matches the target melody note.
4. **Fill the Gaps:** Connect target notes using single-note melody runs, 2-note intervals (3rds or 6ths), or chromatic approach chords.
5. **Adjust Bass & Rhythm:** Introduce bass motion or syncopation (stabbing chords off the beat, letting notes sustain) so the arrangement feels rhythmic rather than stiff.

## Worked Example 1

George Gershwin's **"But Not For Me"** is an ideal choice. It has a clean 32-bar $A_1-B-A_2-C$ structure with clear, step-wise melodic moves and room for nice voice leading.

To make this practical, let's arrange the **first 8 bars** (the $A_1$ section) in the standard jam key of **F Major**.

---

### Step 1: The Lead Sheet & Target Notes

Here is the basic chord progression and melody movement for the first 8 bars in F Major:

| Bar | Chords | Melody Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | $Fmaj7$ | **C** (hold) |
| **2** | $Fmaj7$ | C $\rightarrow$ B $\rightarrow$ C $\rightarrow$ D |
| **3** | $Gm7$ | **F** (hold) |
| **4** | $C7$ | E $\rightarrow$ F $\rightarrow$ G |
| **5** | $Fmaj7$ | **A** (hold) |
| **6** | $F7$ or $D7$ | A $\rightarrow$ G# $\rightarrow$ A $\rightarrow$ B |
| **7** | $Gm7$ | **C** (hold) |
| **8** | $C7$ | A $\rightarrow$ G $\rightarrow$ F |

Notice the **target notes** on beat 1 of bars 1, 3, 5, and 7: $C$, $F$, $A$, and $C$. These hold points carry the main harmonic weight where we build full voicings.

---

### Step 2: Selecting Voicings for Key Target Points

#### Bar 1: $Fmaj7$ with $C$ on Top

* **Melody Note:** $C$ (5th string / 8th fret or 2nd string / 1st fret). To keep harmony open below, we put the melody on the **1st string, 8th fret ($C$)**.
* **Voicing Choice:** **Drop-2 $Fmaj7$** on strings 4-3-2-1.
* **Fretboard Shape:** `x - 7 - 9 - 9 - 10 - 8`
* *Notes:* $F$ (4th string / 7th), $A$ (3rd string / 9th), $E$ (2nd string / 9th), $C$ (1st string / 8th).
* *Function:* Root on 4th string, 3rd, Major 7th, with Melody ($C$) on top.



#### Bar 3: $Gm7$ with $F$ on Top

* **Melody Note:** $F$ (1st string, 1st fret, or 2nd string, 6th fret). Let's use the **1st string, 1st fret ($F$)** or slide down to string 2. If we keep it in lower position:
* **Voicing Choice:** **Shell / 3-Note $Gm7$** on strings 6-4-3-1.
* **Fretboard Shape:** `3 - x - 3 - 3 - x - 1`
* *Notes:* $G$ (6th string / 3rd), $F$ (4th string / 3rd), $B\flat$ (3rd string / 3rd), $F$ (1st string / 1st).
* *Function:* Simple root, $b7$, $b3$, with Melody ($F$) on top.



#### Bar 5: $Fmaj7$ with $A$ on Top

* **Melody Note:** $A$ (1st string, 5th fret).
* **Voicing Choice:** **Drop-3 $Fmaj7$** or **Rootless $Fmaj7$ (or $Am7$ shell)**.
* **Fretboard Shape:** `1 - x - 2 - 2 - 1 - 5` or `x - 8 - 7 - 5 - 5 - 5`
* *Root-on-6 option:* $F$ (6th / 1st), $E$ (4th / 2nd), $A$ (3rd / 2nd), $C$ (2nd / 1st), $A$ (1st / 5th).



---

### Step 3: Connecting the Gaps (Bars 2 & 4)

Instead of clamping down on a heavy chord for every single note in bars 2 and 4, we use **light comping + single-note fills**:

* **Bar 2:** Hit the $Fmaj7$ on beat 1. On beats 2–4 ($C \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \rightarrow D$), play the line on the 2nd string while holding the bass note ($F$), or play 2-note 3rds/6ths underneath.
* **Bar 4:** Hit $C7$ (e.g., shell `x - 3 - 2 - 3 - x - x`) on beat 1, then play $E \rightarrow F \rightarrow G$ as single melody notes leading up to the $A$ in bar 5.

---

### Step 4: Adding Color & Secondary Dominants (Bars 6–8)

In Bar 6, the standard leads into $Gm7$ (Bar 7). To create stronger forward momentum, turn the second half of Bar 6 into a **secondary dominant ($D7$)** or a **diminished passing chord ($G\sharp^\circ7$)**:

* **Bar 6 Choice:** $D7(\flat9)$ leading to $Gm7$.
* Melody note is $A$ moving to $G\sharp \rightarrow A \rightarrow B\flat$.
* Put a **$D7(\flat9)$ shape** under that $A$: `x - 5 - 4 - 5 - 4 - 5` ($D - F\sharp - C - E\flat - A$).



---

### Next Steps to Try on Guitar

1. Grab an $Fmaj7$ shape where **$C$ is on the 1st string** (8th fret or open string).
2. Grab a $Gm7$ shape where **$F$ is on top**.
3. Grab an $Fmaj7$ shape where **$A$ is on top** (1st string, 5th fret).

Once those three anchor points feel comfortable under your fingers, you simply fill the spaces between them with single-note lines or 2-note shell fragments


## Example 2 Approaches to arranging Autumn Leaves

Beyond a single basic arrangement, **"Autumn Leaves"** is one of the most versatile canvas tunes in the jazz repertoire. Because its harmonic framework is pure $ii - V - I - IV$ (Major) followed by $ii^\varnothing - V - i$ (Minor), you can apply completely different arranging philosophies depending on the mood, tempo, and guitar format (solo guitar, duo, or trio).

Here are the primary macro-approaches used by master jazz guitarists to arrange "Autumn Leaves."

---

### 1. The "Joe Pass" Walking Bass & Comp Style

**Best for:** Medium-to-fast swing, solo guitar performance without a bassist.

Instead of playing static block chords, the left hand splits into two roles: keeping a steady $4/4$ walking bass line on the bottom strings while stabbing chord fragments and single-note melody phrases on top.

* **Technique:**
* The bass plays scale tones, chromatic approaches, and dominant enclosures on beats 1, 2, 3, 4.
* The melody and chord pops live primarily on the **and** of beats (syncopation) to keep the rhythm driving.


* **How it applies to Autumn Leaves:**
* Over **$Cm7 \rightarrow F7$**, the bass walks $C - D - E\flat - E\natural$ directly into $F$ on beat 1 of the next bar.
* When the melody sustains on long notes (like the $F$ in Bar 1 or $D$ in Bar 3), the bass line continues moving beneath it, filling the dead space so the groove never stops.



```text
  Cm7                      F7
  Beat: 1   2   3   4      1   2   3   4
E|------1-------1--------|--------------------|
B|------1-------1--------|-----1--------------|  <-- Melody & Chord hits (syncopated)
G|------3-------3--------|-----2--------------|
D|-----------------------|--------------------|
A|----3---3---1----------|---1---2------------|  <-- Walking Bass (beats 1, 2, 3, 4)
E|------------------4----|--------------------|

```

---

### 2. The Modern Contrapuntal Style (Two-Voice Motion)

**Best for:** Rubato intros, introspective ballad arrangements, and classical-adjacent interpretations (e.g., Ted Greene, Ralph Towner).

Instead of thinking in 4-note chord stacks, you view the guitar strictly as **two independent voices**: a high melody line and a low counter-melody line moving in contrary or oblique motion.

* **Technique:**
* Avoid thick 4- or 5-note voicings. Stick almost entirely to **intervals of 3rds, 6ths, and 10ths**.
* Use **contrary motion**: as the melody moves *down* ($F \rightarrow E\flat \rightarrow D \rightarrow C$), the bass line moves *up* ($A \rightarrow B\flat \rightarrow C \rightarrow D$).


* **How it applies to Autumn Leaves:**
* In Bars 1–2, as the melody descends $F \rightarrow E\flat \rightarrow D \rightarrow C$, play a rising inner voice ($E\flat \rightarrow F \rightarrow F\sharp \rightarrow G$). The resulting tension creates rich, classical-sounding voice leading without cluttering the fretboard.



---

### 3. The "George Shearing" / Wes Montgomery Block-Chord Style

**Best for:** Shout choruses, high-energy trio playing, and big, harmonically dense choruses.

Every single melody note—even fast pickup notes—gets harmonized as a full 4-note or 5-note chord block. The melody becomes the top voice of a locked, parallel-moving horn section.

* **Technique:**
* Uses **Drop-2 voicings** or **4-Way Close** structures locked in parallel motion.
* Non-harmonic melody notes are harmonized using **Diminished 7th passing chords**.


* **How it applies to Autumn Leaves:**
* On the pickup line ($C - D - E\flat$), instead of single notes, play three consecutive chord shapes moving up the neck, resolving into the $Cm7$ on beat 1.
* Gives a massive, orchestrational sound—though it requires fast hand movements and precise hybrid/fingerstyle picking to hit all strings simultaneously.



---

### 4. Pedal Points & Modal Re-Harmonization

**Best for:** Modern jazz, ECM-style arrangements, atmospheric intros/outros (e.g., Kurt Rosenwinkel, Lage Lund).

Rather than playing standard root-position chords for every bar, you anchor a **static bass note (pedal point)** underneath changing upper-structure harmonies, or apply re-harmonization over the functional $ii - V$ movements.

* **Technique:**
* **Tonic/Dominant Pedal:** Hold an open $D$ or low $G$ string through the first 4 bars while the $Cm7 - F7 - B\flat maj7 - E\flat maj7$ voicings shift above it.
* **Tritone Substitutions & Altered Dominants:** Replace standard dominant chords with their tritone equivalents or colortones.


* **How it applies to Autumn Leaves:**
* **Bar 2 ($F7$):** Replace $F7$ with $B7(\sharp11)$ moving down a half-step into $B\flat maj7$.
* **Bar 6 ($D7$):** Replace $D7$ with an $A\flat7alt$ or a $G\sharp^\circ7$ over an $E$ bass note to create dark, unexpected harmonic tension before resolving to $Gm7$.



---

### Comparison of Arranging Approaches

| Approach | Primary Texture | Difficulty | Harmonic Flavor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Traditional Shell / Drop-2** | Chord on beat 1 + single-note fills | Low–Medium | Standard Real Book / Classic Swing |
| **Walking Bass & Comp** | Steady 4/4 bass + syncopated chords | Medium–High | Joe Pass / Tuck Andress / Stride |
| **Contrapuntal (2-Voice)** | 3rds, 6ths, 10ths, contrary motion | Medium | Ted Greene / Chamber / Baroque |
| **Block Chords** | Locked 4-note chords on every note | High | Wes Montgomery / Big Band |
| **Pedal Point / Modern** | Open strings, tritone subs, altered extensions | Medium–High | ECM / Modern Contemporary |

---

### How to Structurally Build a Complete Performance

When performing "Autumn Leaves" solo, you don't have to stick to just one style. A professional arrangement usually stacks these techniques across multiple choruses to create a narrative arc:

1. **Intro:** Rubato contrapuntal movement or pedal-point drone over the $A$ section motif.
2. **Chorus 1 (Head):** Clean, spacious Shell/Drop-2 arrangement focusing on clear melody presentation.
3. **Chorus 2 (Solo/Development):** Single-note improvised lines mixed with walking bass or octave-doubled melody lines (Wes style).
4. **Chorus 3 (Climax):** Block-chord / Locked-hands harmonization over the main theme.
5. **Outro:** Retardando on the final $ii^\varnothing - V - i$ cadence with voice-led sub-dominant alterations, resolving to a lush $Gm(maj7)$ or $G2$ voicing.
